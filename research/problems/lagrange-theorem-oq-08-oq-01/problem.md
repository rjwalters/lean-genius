# Problem: A₄ has order 12 but no subgroup of order 6 — sharpness of Lagrange's converse failure

## Problem Statement

### Formal Statement

$$
|A_4| = 12, \quad 6 \mid 12, \quad \text{yet } A_4 \text{ has no subgroup } H \text{ with } |H| = 6.
$$

Formalize this explicit witness certifying that the converse of Lagrange's theorem fails: a divisor
`d` of `|G|` need not be the order of any subgroup. Concretely, prove in Lean that
`Alternating (Fin 4)` has cardinality 12 and that there is **no** subgroup of order 6.

### Plain Language

Lagrange's theorem says the order of any subgroup divides the order of the group. The converse —
"for every divisor `d` of `|G|` there is a subgroup of order `d`" — is false. The classic minimal
counterexample is the alternating group `A₄` (rotations of the tetrahedron): it has 12 elements and
6 divides 12, but `A₄` has no subgroup of order 6. (Such a subgroup would have index 2, hence be
normal, and would force an element of order 6 or a specific structure that `A₄` lacks; concretely
`A₄`'s only proper nontrivial normal subgroup is the Klein four-group `V₄` of order 4.)

### Why This Matters

The parent entry `lagrange-theorem-oq-08` discusses the converse and its failure; this open
question asks for the *explicit certified witness* rather than an abstract existence statement.
A machine-checked `A₄`-has-no-order-6-subgroup lemma is a reusable, pedagogically valuable object
and a natural companion to Cauchy's theorem and the Sylow theorems (which give *partial* converses).

## Known Results

### What's Already Proven

- Lagrange's theorem (parent family) — subgroup order divides group order.
- Cauchy's theorem (Mathlib `exists_prime_orderOf_dvd_card`) — a prime divisor of `|G|` is realized
  by an element (hence a cyclic subgroup) of that order. This is the *prime* converse; the composite
  case (6) is exactly where it fails.
- Mathlib knows `A₄`: `alternatingGroup (Fin 4)`, its cardinality, and facts about `V₄`.

### What's Still Open

- The explicit "no subgroup of order 6" certificate for `A₄` in this gallery (this problem).

### Our Goal

Prove two facts in Lean: (1) `Fintype.card (alternatingGroup (Fin 4)) = 12`, and (2)
`¬ ∃ H : Subgroup (alternatingGroup (Fin 4)), Fintype.card H = 6`. The second is the substance;
approach it via the index-2-implies-normal argument or by a finite computation over the subgroup
lattice.

## Initial Thoughts

### Potential Approaches

1. **Approach A**: Index-2 normality argument.
   - Why it might work: a subgroup of order 6 in `A₄` has index 2, hence is normal; a normal
     subgroup of order 6 would contain all 3-cycles (there are 8, but 8 > 6) — contradiction, since
     `A₄` is generated by its 3-cycles and a normal subgroup containing one 3-cycle contains all of
     a conjugacy class. Mathlib has `Subgroup.normal_of_index_eq_two`.
   - Risk: assembling the conjugacy-class counting of 3-cycles may need some manual combinatorics.

2. **Approach B**: `Decidable`/`decide` over the finite subgroup structure.
   - Why it might work: `A₄` is a concrete finite group; membership and subgroup order are
     decidable, so a finite search can in principle certify no order-6 subgroup exists.
   - Risk: `decide`/`native_decide` on `Subgroup` search may be infeasible or pull in
     `Lean.ofReduceBool` (axiom cost) — prefer the structural Approach A for a 0-axiom result.

### Key Difficulties

- Counting 3-cycles (there are 8) and using that a normal subgroup is a union of conjugacy classes
  to derive the contradiction.
- Keeping the proof `decide`-free to preserve a verified (0-axiom) status.

### What Would a Proof Need?

- Key lemma 1: `Subgroup.normal_of_index_eq_two` and the index computation `[A₄ : H] = 2`.
- Key lemma 2: a normal subgroup is a union of conjugacy classes; the 3-cycle class has size 8.
- Technical requirements: `alternatingGroup`, `Equiv.Perm`, conjugacy classes of 3-cycles.
