## Problem Statement

### Formal Statement

$$
\text{Each Platonic solid } P \text{ has full symmetry group } \mathrm{Sym}(P) \cong W(\Phi),
$$

a finite reflection (Coxeter) group: tetrahedron $\to A_3 \cong S_4$ (order 24),
cube/octahedron $\to B_3$ (order 48), dodecahedron/icosahedron $\to H_3$ (order 120).
Concretely, formalize that the symmetry group of each Platonic solid is generated by
reflections and is isomorphic to the corresponding Coxeter group, and that the dual
pairs share a symmetry group.

### Plain Language

There are exactly five Platonic solids, and they fall into three symmetry classes
(tetrahedral, octahedral, icosahedral) because dual solids share symmetries. Each
class is a finite *reflection group* — a group generated by mirror reflections,
classified by Coxeter as $A_3$, $B_3$, $H_3$. The goal is to make this correspondence
precise and, where feasible, machine-checked.

### Why This Matters

It links the classical classification of regular polyhedra (Wiedijk #100 territory)
to the modern theory of finite reflection groups and root systems. Formalizing even
the order counts and the dual-pair coincidences strengthens the gallery's geometry/
group-theory bridge and motivates Coxeter-group infrastructure in Lean.

## Known Results

### What's Already Proven

- There are exactly five Platonic solids — `platonic-solids` (gallery parent).
- Finite-group order/structure tools — Mathlib `GroupTheory`, `Equiv.Perm`, `S₄`.
- Root systems / Coxeter groups — partial Mathlib support (`Mathlib.LinearAlgebra.RootSystem`).

### What's Still Open

- A formal definition of $\mathrm{Sym}(P)$ for each solid as a subgroup of $O(3)$.
- Isomorphisms $\mathrm{Sym}(\text{tet}) \cong S_4$, $\mathrm{Sym}(\text{cube}) \cong S_4 \times C_2$, etc.
- Identification with abstract Coxeter groups $A_3, B_3, H_3$.

### Our Goal

Pick a tractable slice: formalize the *rotation* symmetry groups and their orders for
the three classes (12, 24, 60) and/or the full groups (24, 48, 120), and state the
dual-pair coincidence. Full Coxeter-isomorphism is a stretch goal.

## Initial Thoughts

### Potential Approaches

1. **Approach A — Combinatorial symmetry groups**: Define each solid by its face/vertex
   incidence structure and take the automorphism group of that structure; compute
   orders via orbit–stabilizer (e.g., tetrahedron: 4 faces × 3 rotations = 12 rotations).
   - Why it might work: avoids hard $O(3)$ geometry; orbit-stabilizer is in Mathlib.
   - Risk: relating combinatorial automorphisms to *geometric* isometries needs care.

2. **Approach B — Concrete matrix groups in $O(3)$**: Represent each symmetry group as
   an explicit finite subgroup of $3\times 3$ orthogonal matrices and identify it.
   - Why it might work: fully faithful to the geometry.
   - Risk: heavy linear-algebra/decidability burden, especially for $H_3$ (golden ratio).

### Key Difficulties

- Bridging combinatorial automorphisms and geometric isometries.
- Coxeter/root-system support in Mathlib is still developing; $H_3$ is non-crystallographic.

### What Would a Proof Need?

- Key lemma 1: orbit–stabilizer order computation for each solid's symmetry action.
- Key lemma 2: dual solids induce the same symmetry group.
- Technical requirements: finite group theory; (stretch) Coxeter-group definitions.

## Tractability Assessment

**Difficulty**: Medium–High

**Justification**:
- Order computations via orbit–stabilizer are very tractable in Mathlib.
- The Coxeter-isomorphism statements ($H_3$ especially) are harder and may exceed
  current Mathlib infrastructure.
- A scoped result (rotation-group orders + dual coincidence) is achievable.

**Estimated Effort**:
- Exploration: 1–2 days
- If tractable (orders + duals): 1–2 weeks
- If hard (full Coxeter iso): unknown
