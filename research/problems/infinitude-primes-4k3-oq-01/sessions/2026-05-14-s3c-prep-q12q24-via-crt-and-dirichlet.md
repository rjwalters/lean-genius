## 1. The obstruction for `q = 12`

`(ℤ/12)ˣ = {1, 5, 7, 11}` has order 4, isomorphic to the Klein four-group `K₄ = ℤ/2 × ℤ/2`. Unlike `q = 8`, the Klein-4 structure for `q = 12` is **not generated by a single quadratic-residue character**: the natural CRT decomposition

    (ℤ/12)ˣ ≅ (ℤ/4)ˣ × (ℤ/3)ˣ ≅ ℤ/2 × ℤ/2

reveals that `p ≡ 11 (mod 12)` is equivalent to **the simultaneous condition**

    p ≡ 3 (mod 4)  ∧  p ≡ 2 (mod 3).

The classical `N = 12 · ∏ p_i − 1` construction yields `N ≡ 11 (mod 12)` but does NOT force any single prime factor of `N` to be `≡ 11 (mod 12)`. Concrete counterexample: `N = 35 = 5 · 7`; `35 ≡ 11 (mod 12)`, but `5 ≡ 5 (mod 12)` and `7 ≡ 7 (mod 12)` — neither factor is `≡ 11`.

The standard fix is a **two-character constraint** forcing prime factors of (a divisor of) `N` to simultaneously satisfy two quadratic-residue conditions — one for each prime power in `12 = 2² · 3`. This requires `(−1/p)` and `(−3/p)` analysis (or equivalently `(−1/p)` and `(3/p)`).

## 2. The obstruction for `q = 24`

`(ℤ/24)ˣ` has order `φ(24) = φ(8) · φ(3) = 4 · 2 = 8`. By CRT:

    (ℤ/24)ˣ ≅ (ℤ/8)ˣ × (ℤ/3)ˣ ≅ K₄ × ℤ/2.

So `p ≡ 23 (mod 24)` is equivalent to

    p ≡ 7 (mod 8)  ∧  p ≡ 2 (mod 3).

Compared to `q = 12`, this requires **three** simultaneous residue conditions (encoded as one Klein-4 + one Klein-2 character): two from `(ℤ/8)ˣ` (since `p ≡ 7 (mod 8)` is itself two simultaneous quadratic-residue conditions per S3b PREP §3) plus one from `(ℤ/3)ˣ`.

## 3. Two routes to discharge S3c

### Route-A — Classical Schur-style elementary construction

For `q = 12`, the relevant classical construction (attributed variously to Schur (1912) and Murty–Thain (2006)) uses the polynomial identity for the 12th cyclotomic polynomial `Φ₁₂(x) = x⁴ − x² + 1`, evaluated at a suitable form to extract primes with the desired joint residue profile.

A cleaner two-character variant for `q = 12, a = 11`:

> Let `p_1, ..., p_n` be the assumed-finite primes `≡ 11 (mod 12)`. Define
> ```
> N = (4 · ∏ p_i)² · 3 − 4 = 12 · ((4 · ∏ p_i)² · (1/4) − 1/3)
> ```
> (formal version with care for `Nat` denominators). The construction forces:
>   (i) `(N · k)` has every odd prime factor satisfying `(−1/p) = −1` (i.e. `p ≡ 3 mod 4`);
>   (ii) every odd prime factor satisfying `(−3/p) = −1` (i.e. `p ≡ 2 mod 3`).
>
> Combined: every odd prime factor of (a divisor of) `N` is `≡ 11 (mod 12)`. A standard parity argument (analogous to S3b §3 Step 3) then extracts at least one prime factor that is NOT in the original list.

**Honest caveat**: I have not verified the exact `N` formula against a definitive textbook source. Murty's book *Problems in Analytic Number Theory* (2nd ed., Springer 2008) §1.2 discusses Schur's theorem in general but states the construction abstractly. Murty–Thain *Prime numbers in certain arithmetic progressions* (Functiones et Approximatio 35 (2006), 249–259) proves that Euclid-style elementary proofs exist exactly when `a² ≡ 1 (mod q)` (i.e. `a ∈ {±1, …}` among the involutions of `(ℤ/q)ˣ`), but the explicit construction for `q ∈ {12, 24}` is left as case-by-case work in their proof of the main theorem.

**Risk for S3c ACT via Route-A**: HIGH. The Lean formalisation of a two-character constraint argument is significantly more intricate than the single-character `q = 8` case (S3b PREP estimated ~150 LOC for `q = 8`; Route-A for `q = 12` likely ~300+ LOC).

For `q = 24, a = 23` via Route-A: even harder. Three simultaneous residue conditions × the parity argument ⇒ estimated ~400+ LOC. Recommendation: DO NOT pursue Route-A for `q = 24` in S3c ACT.
